Computer Science & Information Retrieval

How Search Engines Evolved: From Keywords to PageRank

Explore the core concepts behind early search engines, web spam manipulation, and the mathematical breakthrough made at Stanford University in 1998.

How Early Search Engines Ranked Websites (1990–1997)

First-generation search engines like WebCrawler, Lycos, Excite, and AltaVista treated the World Wide Web like a giant digital library catalog. When a user typed a query, the engine scanned its inverted index to find pages containing those exact words.

Term Frequency (TF)

If Page A repeated the word "artificial intelligence" 25 times and Page B mentioned it 3 times, Page A was assumed to be much more relevant—even if Page A had zero useful information.

On-Page Meta Tags

Early engines heavily trusted HTML <title> and <meta name="keywords"> tags written directly by the website owner.

Why Keyword-Based Ranking Could Be Easily Manipulated

Because early algorithms only examined what a webpage claimed about itself, dishonest website operators quickly discovered how to game the system—a practice known as Spamdexing.

  • Keyword Stuffing: Repeating popular search terms dozens of times in titles, descriptions, and paragraphs (try Preset #1 above to see ai-cheap-deals.biz take #1 in Old Search!).
  • Invisible Text: Writing white text on a white background filled with hundreds of unrelated trending keywords so humans couldn't see it, but search crawlers indexed it.
  • Meta-Tag Hijacking: Copying the keywords of famous brands or universities into unrelated websites to steal search traffic.

How Larry Page and Sergey Brin Introduced PageRank (1998)

While Ph.D. students at Stanford University, Larry Page and Sergey Brin realized that academic research papers already solved a similar problem: a paper's importance isn't judged by how many times the author repeats a word, but by how many other respected papers cite it.

The "Random Surfer" Intuition

Imagine a user randomly clicking hyperlinks on the web. With probability d = 0.85 (the damping factor), they click a random outgoing link on the current page. With probability 1 - d = 0.15, they get bored and jump to a completely random webpage. A page's PageRank measures the long-run probability that this random surfer lands on that page!

Why Links Act as Signals of Website Importance

In PageRank, a hyperlink from Website Ti to Website A acts as a vote of confidence. However, not all votes are equal:

PR(A) = (1 - d) + d × Σ ( PR(Ti) / C(Ti) )
1. Voter Authority: PR(Ti)

A link from a prestigious site like Stanford or Wikipedia passes far more authority than a link from an obscure blog nobody links to.

2. Link Dilution: 1 / C(Ti)

If a hub links to 100 websites, its PageRank vote is divided by 100. A curated page linking to only 2 sites passes half its rank to each.

3. Dangling Nodes

Pages with 0 outgoing links are "dead ends." In the random surfer model, their PageRank is redistributed evenly across all pages so rank doesn't leak out of the graph.

How PageRank Differs from Overall Modern Google Ranking

While PageRank remains a foundational component of Google's link analysis, modern Google Search evaluates hundreds of signals in milliseconds:

Semantic AI (BERT & MUM)

Modern neural language models understand intent, synonyms, and context rather than requiring exact keyword matches.

E-E-A-T & SpamBrain

Google evaluates Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness, actively neutralizing paid link schemes.

Page Experience & Mobile

Core Web Vitals (loading speed, visual stability), HTTPS security, and mobile-friendliness directly influence rankings.

Query + Link Fusion

Pure PageRank is query-independent (global authority). Modern search combines query relevance with link authority and freshness.
